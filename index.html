How warmer waters may be fueling stronger Florida hurricanes

By Georgia Ruffolo

There are few places in the world that know hurricanes like Florida. With more than 1,200 miles of coastline along the Atlantic Ocean and the Gulf of Mexico, the state often finds itself in the midst of these storms, and every stretch of its coast has been hit by at least one major storm since 1850. But over the last couple of decades, the story has shifted from simply “how frequent do storms hit?” to “how ferocious — and how costly — are they when they do?”

Since 1980, Florida has faced about 41% of all U.S. hurricanes and an estimated 450 billion dollars in cumulative disaster costs, leading all fifty states.[26] Hurricanes have become some of the most expensive disasters in American history; Hurricane Ian, which struck southwest Florida in 2022, is now considered the costliest hurricane in Florida’s history, with estimated losses around 112 billion dollars.[22] Those price tags matter for homeowners, local governments, and insurers, but they also suggest a deeper question: what, exactly, is making some of these storms so intense?

One place scientists increasingly look for answers is just offshore, in the water itself. Sea surface temperatures — the temperature of the top layers of the ocean — have been climbing for decades. In South Florida, a regional climate indicator based on NOAA’s long‑term sea surface temperature record shows that summertime ocean temperatures around the tip of the peninsula have warmed by about 0.49 °F per decade from 1982 to 2024.[14] That may sound pretty small, but over multiple decades, it adds up to a noticeably warmer foundation for any storm moving over the region.

Florida’s hurricane pattern, in context

To understand how Florida’s hurricanes have changed, I visited NOAA’s official hurricane database, often called HURDAT2, and a summary of “Continental United States Hurricane Impacts/Landfalls” that lists which storms struck each state.[2] These records track storm names, dates, paths, maximum sustained winds, and Saffir–Simpson category at landfall, which allows for a clean comparison of how many hurricanes hit Florida each year and how strong they were when they did.

Looking across the last twenty‑plus years, the pattern is uneven, but telling. Florida has seen very active periods — such as the mid‑2000s, when multiple hurricanes made landfall in back‑to‑back seasons — and calmer stretches with fewer direct hits but still damaging storms.[2][9] The big picture from recent climate research, though, is that while the number of tropical cyclones varies quite a bit from year to year, a larger share of the storms that do form are now reaching major hurricane strength (Category 3 or higher). In other words, intensity is trending up, even when storm counts bounce around.[13]

For Floridians, that matters far more than abstract global averages. On the ground, higher‑category landfalls translate into stronger winds, denser storm surge, and a bigger gap between what homes and infrastructure were designed to handle and what the storm actually delivers.

What’s happening in the water?

The ocean side of this story leans on NOAA’s Optimum Interpolation Sea Surface Temperature (OISST) dataset, a long‑term climate record that blends satellite, ship, buoy, and float observations into a consistent daily map of global sea surface temperatures.[4] For South Florida, a regional indicator analyzes the summertime (July–September) average SST for waters surrounding the tip of the peninsula, which gives a simple way to track how coastal temperatures have evolved.[14]

From 1982 to 2024, that indicator shows a clear upward trend. Summertime SST in the South Florida region has warmed nearly half a degree Fahrenheit per decade. The same source notes that this ocean trend closely matches the rise in nighttime minimum air temperatures over land — about 0.52 °F per decade — suggesting that the atmosphere and ocean in the region are warming together.[14] So, for you Floridians out there battling the heat, you should know that this isn’t just a hotter‑than‑normal summer. It’s a slow, steady, and serious shift in Florida’s natural environment.

Globally, NASA’s sea surface temperature overview puts typical SSTs in the tropics at more than 86 °F, compared with temperatures near 28 °F close to the poles.[19] Warmer water in the tropics and subtropics is one of the reasons those regions host the world’s most powerful tropical cyclones. Classic work on hurricane formation showed that storms generally need sea surface temperatures above roughly 26–27 °C (around 79–81 °F) to form and become stronger.[5] South Florida’s coastal waters routinely hit and surpass that threshold during summer and early fall, which means any extra warming adds onto an already favorable setting for storms.[14]

How warmer waters can feed stronger storms

Physically, the connection between warmer oceans and stronger hurricanes comes down to energy. Hurricanes draw power from latent heat — energy released when warm, moist air rises from the ocean surface and condenses into clouds. The warmer the ocean surface and upper layer are, the more energy is available to fuel that process. A recent explainer on Atlantic hurricanes notes that sea surface temperatures above 26.5 °C, down to about 50 meters depth, can support a storm’s intensification into a hurricane, and that episodes of extremely high SST — marine heatwaves — have made rapid intensification about 50 percent more likely.[13]

Researchers have also tried to quantify this link more directly. One correlation analysis of sea surface temperature and storm intensity found a positive relationship between higher SST at the time of hurricane events and stronger storms by using regression methods along storm tracks.[11] Another modeling study that looked at future climate scenarios concluded that projected increases in average SST are likely to lead to more intense hurricanes by the end of the twenty‑first century.[7] These studies don’t claim that sea surface temperature explains everything, but they do treat warmer water as a consistent pressure pushing storms toward higher categories.

In Florida, recent storm history offers the best examples. Hurricane Ian in 2022 became the costliest hurricane in the state’s history, with estimated losses around 112 billion dollars, and made landfall near Cayo Costa as a high‑end Category 4 storm.[22] Scientists highlighted unusually warm waters in the eastern Gulf of Mexico as one of the reasons behind Ian’s rapid intensification before making landfall.[13] Other recent storms, including Michael in 2018 and Helene in 2024, also intensified over extremely warm waters before hitting the state.[2][9] Taken together, these cases reinforce what the data suggest: when coastal waters are anomalously hot during peak hurricane season, storms moving over them become stronger.

What the research can — and can’t — say

Even with solid datasets, there are limits to what researchers can deem as certain. Sea surface temperature is only one piece of the hurricane puzzle. Studies that combine SST with other metrics, like sea surface height anomalies, suggest that the depth and structure of the warm layer beneath the surface can be just as important as the top layer of the ocean.[12] Wind shear, atmospheric moisture, storm size and track, and local coastal geography are all essential in shaping how a hurricane evolves and what kind of damage it leaves behind.

On the impact side, this story focuses on storm severity mainly through wind‑based categories because that’s what HURDAT2 tracks most precisely.[2] But rainfall, storm surge, and tornado outbreaks can drastically change how destructive a storm is without necessarily showing up in the category number. For instance, a slow‑moving Category 2 that stalls and floods an entire metro area can cause significantly more damage than a fast‑moving Category 3 that passes through quickly.

Most importantly, correlation does not equal causation. Even if the scatter plot shows that years with warmer South Florida waters tend to coincide with stronger Florida hurricanes, that pattern will still represent just one contributing factor among many. The point here is not to say that sea surface temperature alone “creates” any specific storm, but to show that the ocean is silently raising the baseline temperature for every hurricane season. For a state built along the water, understanding that shift — and planning for storms that are more severe — is becoming just as important as tracking the next system on the radar.
