How warmer waters are fueling stronger Florida hurricanes

By Georgia Ruffolo

As Florida prepares for another hurricane season, residents and forecasters are once again watching both coasts for the next storm. With more than 1,200 miles of coastline along the Atlantic Ocean and the Gulf of Mexico, the state often finds itself in the path of annual hurricanes, and every stretch of its coast has been hit by at least one major storm since 1850. But over the last couple of decades, the serious question is no longer how often these natural disasters happen. Now, it is how catastrophic — and how costly — they become once they make landfall.

Since 1980, Florida has experienced about 41% of all U.S. hurricanes and an estimated $450 billion in cumulative disaster costs, more than any other state. Hurricane Ian, which struck southwest Florida in 2022, is now considered the costliest hurricane in state history and the third-costliest in United States history, with estimated losses around $112 billion. Those numbers are important to homeowners, local governments and insurers, but they also suggest a figurative — and literal — climate question: What on Earth is making these storms so intense?

Scientists increasingly look offshore for part of the answer. Sea surface temperature — the temperature of the ocean’s top layer — has climbed for decades. In South Florida, a regional climate indicator based on the National Oceanic and Atmospheric Administration’s (NOAA) long-term sea surface temperature record shows that summertime ocean temperatures around the tip of the peninsula have warmed by about 0.49 °F per decade from 1982 to 2024. That may sound like a pretty insignificant increase, but over time, that warming creates a hotter baseline for storms moving through the region during the height of hurricane season.

Florida’s hurricane pattern, in context

To understand how Florida’s hurricanes have changed, I analyzed NOAA’s official hurricane database, often called HURDAT2, along with a summary of continental United States hurricane impacts and landfalls. These records track storm names, dates, paths, maximum sustained winds and Saffir–Simpson category at landfall, which allows for a clean comparison of the size and scale of hurricanes that hit Florida each year.

Looking across the last 20-plus years, the data shows an uneven pattern. Florida has seen very active periods — such as the mid-2000s, when multiple hurricanes made landfall in back-to-back seasons — and calmer stretches with fewer direct hits. According to researchers, the number of tropical cyclones varies quite a bit from year to year, and a larger share of the storms that do form are now reaching major hurricane strength (Category 3 or higher). In other words, even when storm counts may vary, their intensity is undoubtedly trending up.

For residents, that matters far more than basic global averages. On the ground, higher-category landfalls look like stronger winds, denser storm surges and a bigger gap between what Florida infrastructure was designed to handle versus what the storm actually delivers.

Dr. Jennifer Collins, a professor at the University of South Florida’s School of Geosciences whose research focuses on extreme weather, described hurricanes as “very large heat engines with extraordinary potential” in conditions already prevalent in Florida’s unique environment. Collins explained that storms become hurricanes when they gather heat and moisture from the ocean’s surface. As that air constantly rises and condenses, it releases energy that fuels a hurricane’s winds and overall circulation. This sometimes allows systems to grow at an extraordinary rate.

“Hurricanes that form over the Gulf [of Mexico] tend to be stronger due to the naturally warmer waters off the west coast of Florida,” Collins said.

The hurricane researcher also added that considering the rise in South Florida sea surface temperatures, especially in the Gulf, is a necessary perspective to maintain to better understand worsening hurricane patterns.

What’s happening in the water?

NOAA’s long-term sea-surface-temperature record combines satellite, ship, buoy and float observations into daily global maps of ocean temperatures. In South Florida, a regional climate indicator tracks the average summertime — July through September — sea surface temperature (SST) in waters surrounding the tip of the peninsula.

From 1982 to 2024, that indicator shows a clear upward trend. South Florida’s summertime SST warmed by nearly half a degree Fahrenheit per decade. The trend almost exactly mirrors the increase in minimum air temperatures over land, which suggests that Florida’s entire ecosystem is warming together.

This is more than a particularly hot summer for Floridians. These changes indicate a gradual shift in Florida’s climate conditions, which happen just as the state enters the time of year when storm systems are a serious threat.

Warm ocean water is a basic ingredient for hurricane formation and intensification. NASA notes that tropical sea surface temperatures can exceed 86 °F, while water near the poles can approach 28 °F. The warm tropical and subtropical oceans help support many of the world’s most powerful cyclones.

Research on hurricane formation has found that storms generally need water temperatures above about 26–27 °C, or 79–81 °F, to develop and strengthen. South Florida’s coastal waters routinely hit and surpass that threshold during summer and early fall, which means any extra warming adds onto an already favorable setting for storms.

How warmer waters feed storms

The connection between warmer oceans and stronger hurricanes comes down to physical energy. Hurricanes draw power from latent heat — energy released when warm, moist air rises from the ocean surface and condenses into clouds. The warmer the ocean surface and upper layer are, the more energy is available to fuel that process. A recent explainer on Atlantic hurricanes noted that sea surface temperatures above 26.5 °C, down to about 50 meters depth, can support a storm’s intensification into a hurricane. The explainer also reported that marine heatwaves have made rapid intensification about 50% more likely.

Researchers have tried to quantify this link more directly. One study using regression analysis found a positive relationship between higher sea-surface temperatures along hurricane tracks and greater storm intensity. Another study modeling future climate conditions concluded that rising average SSTs are likely to produce more intense hurricanes by the end of the 21st century.

While they are telling, neither of these findings confirms that ocean temperature alone determines whether a storm will become a serious hurricane. Instead, the research identifies warmer water as a consistent factor that can increase the potential for storms to gain energy.

Examples of this connection can be found in Florida’s recent hurricane history. Storm research shows that Hurricane Ian strengthened rapidly over the eastern Gulf of Mexico before making landfall near Cayo Costa as a high-end Category 4 hurricane, and reported unusually warm Gulf waters as a factor in Ian’s rapid intensification. Hurricanes Michael in 2018, Helene and Milton in 2024, were also shown to have intensified under the same conditions before affecting Florida’s west coast.

Together, these cases tell us why ocean conditions deserve attention during hurricane season. When a storm crosses abnormally warm water, it may have greater potential to intensify very quickly before landfall, unfortunately leaving those affected less time to prepare for the worst.

What the research can — and can’t — say

Even with solid datasets, there are limits to what research can deem as certain. Sea surface temperature is only one piece of the hurricane paradox. Studies that combine SST with other metrics, like sea surface height anomalies, suggest that the depth and structure of the warm layer beneath the surface can be just as important as the top layer of the ocean. Wind shear, atmospheric moisture, storm size and track, and local coastal geography are also essential in analyzing how a hurricane evolves and what kind of damage it leaves behind.

This story focuses on storm severity mainly through wind-based categories because that’s what HURDAT2 tracks most precisely. But rainfall, storm surge and tornado outbreaks can also drastically change how destructive a storm is without necessarily showing up in the category number. For instance, a slow-moving Category 2 that stalls and floods an entire metro area can cause significantly more damage than a fast-moving Category 3 that passes through quickly.

Most importantly, correlation does not prove causation. Even if warmer South Florida waters tend to coincide with stronger hurricanes, temperature is just one contributing factor among many. A warmer ocean does not create every storm, nor can it solely explain the path or impact of a single hurricane.

The point is that long-term ocean warming is raising the baseline conditions under which hurricanes form and intensify. As Florida moves through hurricane season, understanding these environmental changes is becoming just as important as tracking the next system on the radar.
